A Brief Literature Review of the Development and Use of Multi-Dimensional Kolmogorov-Smirnov Tests

Most commonly used statistical tests and procedures require certain assumptions to hold in order to produce valid results. Generally, assumptions such as independence of observations, random selection, and normality (in the case of certain parametric tests) often suggest the need of preliminary data analysis in order to ensure the sample does indeed meet the stated assumptions. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test (KS Test) is one of the more popular and widely used ECDF tests to check both distributional assumptions and the assumption that data is indeed randomly selected. The KS Test can generally be applied to both one-sample and two-sample data. However, the KS Test (and most other ECDF tests), in its original form, is only valid for one-dimensional data and cannot be applied to multi-dimensional data. As researchers have continued to apply the KS Test though, many have recognized this limitation and developed tools for the expansion of KS Tests to multi-dimensional analysis.

While this summary focuses on the extension of KS Tests to multidimensional data, it is interesting to note that the Cramer-Von Mises Test has also been extended to multiple dimensions with the possible conclusion that its multivariate form is more powerful than that of the corresponding KS Test (Chiu, et al., 2009). While the multidimensional KS and Cramer-Von Mises tests seem to be widely used, there seems to be little research on (and little use of) a multidimensional expansion of the Kuiper and Anderson-Darling ECDF Tests. The extension to the Kuiper at least seems trivial, so it seems rather odd to note the lack of literature using a multidimensional Kuiper Test.

Prior to the use of a multivariate KS Test, the common method was to divide the data into bins and then run a chi-square GOF test. Obviously, this is not ideal since it treats a continuous variable as a categorical variable. The seminal work on the expansion of the KS Test was performed by Fasano and Franceschini (Fasano, et al., 1987). They note that a preceding two-dimensional test by Peacock in 1983 made use of the maximum absolute difference between the observed and predicted cumulative distributions, similar to the KS Test but with using four possible ways to cumulate data based on axis directions instead of the traditional single cumulant value in one-dimensional situations. Fasano and Franceschini’s methodology is very similar to Peacock’s but much simplified and thus significantly more efficient. Fasano and Franceschini only consider \(4n\) quadrants instead of considering all \(4n^2\) proposed by Peacock. Thus cumulants are defined by the following four quadrants: \[(x<X_{i},y<Y_{i}) (x<X_{i},y>Y_{i}) (x>X_{i},y<Y_{i}) (x>X_{i},y>Y_{i})\] The maximum difference in each of the four quadrants is found, and the largest difference across the four quadrants (\(D_{BKS}\)) is then used in the KS Test statistic. The one sample test statistic (\(Z_{n}\)) is now defined identically to a one dimensional KS test. \[Z_{n}=D_{BKS}\sqrt{n}\] The two-sample test is defined similarly: \[Z_{n}=\bar{D}_{BKS}\sqrt{n_{1}n_{2}/(n_{1}+n_{2})}\] The distribution for \(Z_{n}\) and the critical values of \(Z_{n}\) are found using Monte Carlo simulations with a uniform distribution as the parent population (Fasano and Franceschini calculated the distribution of \(Z_{n}\) for a 2D and 3D KS Test). Furthermore, the authors verify using Monte Carlo Simulations that there is no loss of power between this test and the Peacock and that this test is sufficiently distribution-free in its calculations (i.e. that it is valid regardless of the shape of the distribution). The only factor of influence in this test which must be additionally considered is the level of correlation between the two variables. As the correlation approaches 1, the multi-dimensional KS Test statistic behaves much like the one-dimensional KS Test statistic. However, as correlation decreases, the critical value for the test statistic increases above the usual one-dimensional critical value. Thus, the distribution of the test statistic, \(Z_{n}\), is dependent on the correlation coefficient and so calculations of critical values and probabilities must take into account both \(Z_{n}\) and the correlation coefficient in order for the Fasano and Franceschini Test to be a distribution free test. A complication arises in the case of the 3D test since there are multiple parameters which must be taken into account when explaining correlation. Fasano and Franceschini simplify this by using the average of the correlations as the correlation coefficient of interest: \[\bar\rho=\frac{\rho_{x,y}+\rho_{x,z}+\rho_{y,z}}{3}\] Presumably, this simplification suffices for larger dimensions as well, though this has not been tested.

In addition to Fasano and Franceschini’s (1987) version of the multivariate KS Test, Cooke’s Test is another attempt at applying the KS Test to multiple dimensions. Cooke’s Test actually is based upon the Peacock Test and originally claimed to be identical to the Peacock Test but more efficient. Interestingly, Cooke’s Test was found to actually not truly approximate the Peacock Test and simulations demonstrated that the original Peacock Test and Fasano and Franceschini’s Test both produced more stable results than Cooke’s Test (Lopes et al., 2007). Lopes et al. (2007) also looked at a version of the KS Test called the ROOT test, named after the software program “ROOT” which specializes in data analysis in C++ and was originally developed as a tool in the field of particle physics. Lopes et al. notes that the ROOT test (produced in the ROOT software) uses a binning mechanism to overcome multidimensionality and computes its statistic based on the average statistic of the 1D tests on each dimension of the data. Even though the ROOT Test is more simplistic, it has the issue that the bin size drastically changes the results. The ROOT Test, along with Cooke’s Test, had much larger standard error and worse estimates overall compared to the Peacock Test and Fasano and Franceschini’s Test.

More recently, due to the computational intensiveness of a multivariate KS Test, Jiang et al. (2021) developed a method of applying the KS Test to multivariate situations using a simple univariate KS Test. Their research centered around how to use the KS Test for out-of-distribution (OOD) detection in deep learning. The method consists of a concept called “random projection” which takes high dimensional objects and projects them onto the real number line in order to encapsulate the high dimensional information in lower dimensional vectors (in this case 1D). According to Theorem 1 in Jiang et al. (2021) almost any random direction will allow the projections (and therefore the distributions) of X and Y to be distinguishable. Supposedly, this assumption is strengthened if the projection is into a higher dimension, but this is practically unhelpful since only a projection onto the real line allows for the use of the traditional KS Test. Since this methodology reduces the data to one dimension, the authors also applied a Cramer-Von Mises Test and found the results to be comparable to the KS Test. The results of using both random projection and autoencoders to reduce dimensionality was also tested with the result that random projection performs better overall for a given level of dimensionality reduction.

As noted by Jiang et al. (2021), the study of a multivariate KS Test has been sporadic at best (p. 4). However, its continued research and development would have significant impact in fields which use high-dimensional data such as astronomy (where Fasano and Franceschini originally applied their version of the KS Test), meteorology, genomics, etc. Overall, it appears from the literature that Fasano and Franceschini’s version of the multidimensional KS Test performs best when examining data that is multidimensional but has low dimensionality (i.e. two or three dimensions). For significantly higher dimensional data, ways which reduce dimensionality (like random projection) ought to be preferred instead of testing the higher dimensional data directly.

Packages for both the Peacock Test and the Fasano and Franceschini (1987) version of the KS Test are found in R and are titled Peacock.test (Xiao, 2017) and fasano.franceschini.test (Connor et al. 2023). The two versions of the KS Test apply to two-sample multivariate KS Tests.
